# Brevity Principles

Three layers govern every answer. Shape tells you how to structure it. Compression rules tell you how to cut it. Signal discipline tells you what must land early.

Apply them in order: pick your shape first, compress second, check signals third.

---

## The Core Rule

**Answer the question in the first sentence — with a clear noun and verb that resolve it.**

Vague first sentences still violate this rule.

> **Weak:** "Yes, I think that's an interesting direction."
> **Strong:** "Yes, we should launch the enterprise tier."

Everything else — context, background, evidence — is support. If the listener walked away after sentence one, they should still know your answer.

This rule sits above every framework. No shape overrides it.

---

## Layer 1: Answer Shape

Pick one shape before you speak. The decision tree routes you fast.

```
Past event?                              → STAR
Opinion, recommendation, or strength?   → Conclusion → Support (light mode)
Reasoning required?                      → Conclusion → Support (analytical mode)
Process question?                        → Three Steps
About you?                               → Present–Past–Future
Presentation or explanation with parts? → Rule of Three*
Building a presentation from scratch?   → Bow and Arrow first, then Rule of Three or Three Steps
Written communication?                   → Answer first, always
```

*When answering a direct question, use Conclusion → Support. Rule of Three is for explaining or presenting — structuring content for a listener, not defending a position.

**Q&A variants:** For spoken Q&A specifically, two compressed versions of Conclusion → Support are useful:
- **PREP** (Point, Reason, Example, Point) — state your point, give a reason, provide an example, restate the point. Good for opinion questions in fast-moving Q&A.
- **ADD** (Answer, Detailed example, Describe relevance) — answer cleanly, reinforce with an example, connect to why it matters. Good when you need to show relevance quickly.

### STAR
**Use when:** Behavioural interview questions. Past events. "Tell me about a time when..." Retrospectives.

**Structure:**
- **Situation + Task** (10–20%): One or two sentences combined. Context only. Stop.
- **Action** (60–70%): What *you* specifically did. Sequential steps, not a list of qualities.
- **Result** (10–20%): What changed. Quantify when possible.

**Two discipline rules:**

First — spend your words on Action. If the listener cannot tell what *you* personally did — not the team, not the context — the answer failed.

Second — actions must be sequential, not listed. Sequence creates credibility. A list of qualities sounds like a CV.

| Weak (list) | Strong (sequence) |
|---|---|
| "I aligned stakeholders, improved communication, and helped execute." | "First I mapped every stakeholder to their objection. Then I ran four targeted conversations. Finally I set a weekly review to prevent drift." |

**Failure mode:** Narrating the environment for sixty seconds and never reaching what you did.

> **Weak:** "So at my last company, we were going through a reorganisation, and the team had been working on this project for about six months, and there were a lot of different stakeholders involved, and the deadline kept moving..."
>
> **Strong:** "We were three weeks from launch with broken stakeholder alignment. I mapped every decision-maker to their concern, ran four targeted conversations in two days, and got sign-off. We shipped on time."

### Conclusion → Support

**Use when:** Any question that asks for your opinion, recommendation, view, or reasoning. This shape handles both quick takes and analytical answers — the mode determines the depth.

**Light mode** — for opinions, strengths, fit questions:
- **Conclusion:** One sentence. Your answer, stated directly.
- **Pillars:** Two supporting reasons, examples, or angles. Default to two. Three is acceptable. Never four — that's a list, not an argument.

**Analytical mode** — for strategic reasoning, "why do you think X?", evaluative questions:
- **Conclusion:** Your position in one sentence.
- **Reason:** The underlying logic. Why it's true.
- **Evidence:** A fact, number, or example that proves the reason.
- **Implication:** What follows. What should happen next, or why it matters.

**How to choose the mode:** If the question asks what you think, use light mode. If it asks why you think it or how you'd evaluate it, use analytical mode.

**The discipline:** In both modes, the conclusion comes first. Evidence never precedes the claim it supports. In analytical mode, the implication belongs near the top when the listener needs to act on it — don't bury "so what" at the end.

**Failure mode (light):** Four or five pillars at equal weight, none landing.

> **Weak:** "I think there are a lot of reasons I'd be strong here. I have experience in X, and also Y, and I've worked in fast-paced environments, and I'm good at stakeholder management, and I enjoy ambiguity..."
>
> **Strong:** "I'd be strong here for two reasons. I've built this specific function from scratch before, so I know where the traps are. And I've done it at exactly your growth stage — which is a different problem than doing it at scale."

**Failure mode (analytical):** Evidence without a claim, leaving the listener to infer your point.

> **Weak:** "Churn has gone up, NPS has declined, support tickets are up 40%, and customers keep mentioning onboarding in exit surveys..."
>
> **Strong:** "The retention problem is onboarding, not the product. Customers who complete onboarding in week one churn at half the rate of those who don't. That means the highest-leverage fix is a structured week-one sequence, not another feature."

### Three Steps
**Use when:** Process questions. "How would you approach X?" "Walk me through your method." 90-day plans. Anything with a natural before/during/after.

**Structure:**
- Three stages, each verb-led.
- Each step enables the next. A progression, not a list.

**The discipline:** Every step starts with a verb. Verbs are trackable. Nouns are not.

| Weak (noun-led) | Strong (verb-led) |
|---|---|
| Discovery | Understand the problem |
| Alignment | Build shared context |
| Execution | Ship and measure |

**Failure mode:** Listing activities rather than describing a sequence with logic.

> **Weak:** "I'd probably do some research first, and then figure out what the priorities are, and then start executing on things."
>
> **Strong:** "First, I'd listen — no output for thirty days, just interviews and observation. Second, I'd identify the one constraint blocking everything else and get alignment on it. Third, I'd execute one high-visibility win before touching anything structural."

### Present–Past–Future
**Use when:** "Tell me about yourself." "Walk me through your background." Any opener question.

**Structure:**
- **Present:** Who you are and what you do now. One to two sentences.
- **Past:** What built that. The thread that explains how you got here.
- **Future:** Why this direction. What you're moving toward and why this role fits.

**The discipline:** Thirty seconds total. Thread a narrative — don't recite a CV. The future piece is what makes it feel intentional rather than accidental.

**Failure mode:** Delivering your entire career chronologically, starting from university.

> **Weak:** "So I studied economics at uni, and then I joined a consultancy, and I was there for three years, and then I moved into tech, and I've been doing product work for the last few years across a few different companies..."
>
> **Strong:** "I'm a product leader focused on the zero-to-one phase — I've taken three products from concept to first revenue. That came out of five years in consulting where I learned to move fast in ambiguous environments. I'm here because this is the right problem at the right stage for what I want to build next."

### Rule of Three
**Use when:** Presentations. Multi-part explanations. Structuring spoken content for a group. Not for answering a direct question — use Conclusion → Support for that.

**Structure:**
- Signal the number up front: "Three things are going on here."
- Deliver each with a label: "First... Second... Third..."
- Each item parallel in structure.

**The discipline:** Signalling the number creates a container in the listener's head. They know when you're done. This removes the urge to keep adding points.

**Failure mode:** Saying "a few things" and listing seven.

> **Weak:** "There are a bunch of factors here. The market has shifted, and we have resourcing issues, and the competitive landscape has changed, and the timeline is tighter, and there's also the question of..."
>
> **Strong:** "Three things are working against this launch. The market window is tighter than our model assumed. Engineering capacity is thirty percent below plan. And the lead competitor just shipped the feature we were betting would be our differentiator. Each one is manageable. Together, they change the go/no-go calculus."

### Bow and Arrow
**Use when:** Building a presentation from scratch. Any time you have content but no clear message yet. Use this before you pick Rule of Three or Three Steps — it finds the point those shapes will carry.

**Structure:**
- **Arrow:** The one thing the audience should remember. One sentence. If you can't state it in one sentence, it isn't clear yet.
- **Bow:** The supporting structure — story, data, example — that makes the arrow land. Built around the arrow, not before it.

Apply at two levels: the whole talk, and each section. A section without its own arrow doesn't need to exist.

**The discipline:** Extract the arrow before drafting anything. The instinct is to start with structure — slides, headings, bullets. Resist it. Structure built without an arrow organises content instead of making a point. Once the arrow is clear, Rule of Three or Three Steps carries it.

**Failure mode:** Starting with the outline and hoping the point emerges.

> **Weak:** Opening with three agenda items — "Today I'll cover Q3 results, team capacity, and H2 planning" — and letting the audience infer what it all means.
>
> **Strong:** Arrow first: "We have ninety days to fix retention or we miss the year." Then build the bow: what's driving churn, what you've already done, what you need from the room. Every section serves that one sentence.

## Layer 2: Compression Rules

Apply these after picking your shape. They reduce word count without losing meaning.

**Rule 1: If the first sentence doesn't contain the answer, delete it.**
This is stricter than cutting filler phrases — delete the entire opener. Start with the sentence that resolves the question.

**Rule 2: Default to short sentences. One clause per sentence.**
Stack clauses only when they genuinely depend on each other. When in doubt, split.

> **Weak:** "The issue we ran into was that the onboarding flow had multiple drop-off points and because the team optimised for flexibility it created confusion."
>
> **Strong:** "The problem was the onboarding flow. It had too many drop-off points. Flexibility created confusion."

**Rule 3: Actions in STAR must be sequential, not listed.**
"I did X, Y, and Z" is a list. "First X. Then Y. That enabled Z." is a sequence. Sequences are credible. Lists are forgettable.

**Rule 4: Pillars cap at three.** Two is the default. Three is acceptable. Four or more means you haven't decided what matters.

**Rule 5: Steps must be verb-led.** "Understand the problem" not "Problem understanding."

**Rule 6: Examples are optional.** Don't explain the idea and give the context and provide the example. Pick one. Add others only if asked.

**Rule 7: Implication comes early.** In analytical answers, "so what" belongs near the top — not after all the evidence.

**Rule 8: End strong.** The brain exits flow just before the finish line. People taper off, add unnecessary qualifiers, or undercut themselves: "I don't know if that makes sense." Anticipate it. Use a summary prompt to close cleanly — "In summary...", "What I want you to remember is...", "So to wrap up..." — then land the plane. The ending is what people remember.

## Layer 3: Signal Discipline

These govern what must appear early in every answer, regardless of shape.

**Signal 1: Sentence one answers the question — with a noun and verb that resolve it.**
Not a vague gesture toward an answer. A sentence that could stand alone.

**Signal 2: Label the structure early.**
Tell the listener what container you're using before you fill it.

- "Two reasons."
- "Three steps."
- "The short answer is yes."
- "The core issue is onboarding."

One phrase. It doesn't slow the answer down. It makes everything that follows easier to track.

**Signal 3: Your personal action is visible.**
In any past-event answer, it must be clear what *you* did. "We" is fine in context. It cannot be the only subject.

**Signal 4: One breath per idea.**
More than twenty seconds without a natural pause means two ideas have merged. Find the seam. Split there.

**Signal 5: Answer first, then build.**
State your conclusion in the first ten seconds. Then support it. In interviews especially, don't wait to be asked for evidence — give your headline, then immediately follow with the specific detail that backs it. The discipline is sequence: conclusion before proof, always.

**Signal 6: Stay in character.**
Don't leak insecurities. If you stumble, go slightly off-track, or feel uncertain, keep going. The audience cannot see what you feel unless you show them. Every time you say "sorry, that was rambly" or "I don't know if that makes sense," you force that lens onto listeners who hadn't noticed anything. Once you announce the problem, they can't unsee it. The fix: stay in it. Acknowledge nothing. End strong. Let the reaction tell you how it actually landed.

## Quick Reference

| Failure mode | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No structure selected | Started speaking before deciding | Choose shape before opening your mouth |
| Context before answer | Core rule violation | Delete opener, start with the answer |
| Vague first sentence | Noun/verb missing | Name the thing, state the action |
| "We" throughout | Signal 3 violation | Name your specific action |
| Four or five pillars | No constraint applied | Cut to two strongest |
| Actions listed, not sequenced | STAR discipline missed | Rewrite as First / Then / Finally |
| Steps feel like a list | Noun-led steps | Add a verb to each step |
| Long S/T in STAR | Proportion rule ignored | One or two sentences for S+T combined |
| Evidence before conclusion | Signal 5 violation | State headline first, then support it |
| Rambling past twenty seconds | Two ideas merged | Find the seam, split into two sentences |
| Undercutting at the end | End strong violation | Use a summary prompt, land the plane |
| Apologising for stumbling | Character break | Stay in it — they didn't notice until you told them |
| Outline built before arrow | Bow and Arrow skipped | State the one thing first, then build the structure around it |

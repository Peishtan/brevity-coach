# Question Bank

Questions organised by scenario mode and type. Each question includes the default shape, a word target, and a coaching focus — the specific discipline the answer needs to demonstrate.

Word targets are calibration guides, not hard limits. They reflect what a tight, well-structured answer looks like for that question type.

---

## Interview Questions

### Openers
*Default shape: Present–Past–Future*
*Word target: 80–100 words*

These questions invite a full narrative. The failure mode is chronological recitation. The coaching focus is threading a story: present state → what built it → why this direction. Thirty seconds spoken. No life story.

| # | Question | Coaching focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Tell me about yourself. | Present → Past → Future in three moves. Future must explain why this role. |
| 2 | Walk me through your background. | Same as above. Resist the urge to start at university. |
| 3 | How did you get into this field? | Thread the logic. One clear reason, not a list of circumstances. |
| 4 | What brought you to apply for this role? | This is Present–Past–Future compressed. Lead with what you want, then why you're positioned for it. |

---

### Behavioural
*Default shape: STAR*
*Word target: 100–130 words*

These questions ask for a specific past event. The failure mode is narrating the environment instead of the action. The coaching focus is S/T in one or two sentences, Action as a sequential set of steps you personally took, Result quantified where possible.

**Conflict and influence**

| # | Question | Coaching focus |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager. What did you do? | Action must show what you specifically did — not how you felt about it. |
| 6 | Give me an example of a time you had to influence without authority. | Name the specific tactics. Not "I built relationships" — what did you actually do? |
| 7 | Tell me about a time you had to change someone's mind. | Lead with what changed, not the backstory of why they disagreed. |
| 8 | Describe a situation where you had to push back on a decision you disagreed with. | Action must be sequential. What did you do first, then next? |
| 9 | Tell me about a time you had to manage a difficult stakeholder. | Personal actions only. "We aligned" is not an action. |

**Managing underperformance**

| # | Question | Coaching focus |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | Tell me about a time you had to address underperformance in someone on your team. | Lead with what you did, not how long you waited before acting. Sequential actions: what you observed, what you said, what changed. |
| 11 | Describe a situation where someone on your team wasn't meeting expectations. How did you handle it? | Personal actions only. "We had conversations" is not an action. What did you specifically say or do? |
| 12 | Tell me about a time you had to let someone go or manage them out. | Own the decision early. Lead with the action, not the backstory of how it got there. |
| 13 | Give me an example of a time you gave someone feedback that was hard to hear. | What you said specifically, not how you felt about saying it. Result must show what changed — or didn't. |
| 14 | Tell me about a time you had to balance supporting someone's development with holding them accountable. | Two distinct actions: what support looked like, what accountability looked like. Not merged into "I coached them." |

**Delivery under pressure**

| # | Question | Coaching focus |
|---|---|---|
| 15 | Describe a time you had to manage competing priorities under pressure. | State what you cut and why. Not just that you reprioritised. |
| 16 | Tell me about a project that failed. What happened and what did you learn? | Own it in the first sentence. Self-awareness fast, not late. |
| 17 | Tell me about a time you had to make a decision with incomplete information. | Decision rationale in one or two sentences. What did you know, what did you assume, what did you decide? |
| 18 | Describe a situation where you had to deliver bad news to a stakeholder. | Lead with what you did, not how uncomfortable it was. |
| 19 | Tell me about a time you had to deliver results under significant constraints. | What constraint, what you did about it, what the result was. Tight S/T. |

**Feedback and growth**

| # | Question | Coaching focus |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | Tell me about a time you received critical feedback. How did you respond? | Lead with the feedback received, then the action you took. Not the emotions. |
| 21 | Tell me about a time you had to deliver difficult feedback to someone. | Personal action must be visible. What did you say, specifically? |
| 22 | Give me an example of a time you had to adapt your approach mid-project. | What changed, why, and what you specifically did differently. |

**Initiative and ownership**

| # | Question | Coaching focus |
|---|---|---|
| 23 | Give an example of when you took initiative beyond your role. | Action first. Context second. |
| 24 | Tell me about a time you identified and solved a problem no one else had noticed. | How you identified it is part of the action, not the situation. |
| 25 | Describe a time you had to build something from scratch. | Sequential actions. What did you do first, then next, then after that? |

**Collaboration and leadership**

| # | Question | Coaching focus |
|---|---|---|
| 26 | Tell me about a time you led a team through a difficult period. | What you personally did to lead. Not team outcomes attributed to "we." |
| 27 | Describe a situation where you had to build alignment across teams. | Specific steps you took. Not "I facilitated discussion." |
| 28 | Tell me about a time a project you owned didn't go as planned. | Own the result early. Then the action you took. |

### Strengths and self-awareness
*Default shape: Conclusion → Support (light mode)*
*Word target: 70–90 words*

These questions ask for your view of yourself. The failure mode is listing adjectives or qualities without evidence. The coaching focus is leading with a headline, then supporting it with two specific, concrete examples or observations — not five vague ones.

| # | Question | Coaching focus |
|---|---|---|
| 29 | What's your greatest strength? | Headline first. Then one or two specific examples that prove it. Not a list of qualities. |
| 30 | What's your greatest weakness? | Real weakness, not a humble brag. Evidence that you've worked on it. |
| 31 | What are you most proud of in your career so far? | Lead with the result. Then the context. Not the other way around. |
| 32 | How would your colleagues describe your working style? | Specific and consistent. Two concrete observations. Not generic adjectives. |
| 33 | What kind of environment do you do your best work in? | Concrete. Tie it to something real. "I thrive in ambiguity" needs an example to land. |
| 34 | What do you find most energising about your work? | One specific thing with evidence. Not a list of everything you enjoy. |
| 35 | What do you find most draining? | Honest answer, briefly. One sentence. Then move on. |
| 36 | How do you handle pressure or stress? | Specific behaviour or system you use. Not "I stay calm." |

---

### Motivation and fit
*Default shape: Conclusion → Support (light mode)*
*Word target: 70–90 words*

These questions probe whether you've thought carefully about this role and company. The failure mode is generic praise or backward-looking negativity. The coaching focus is forward-facing answers with specific signals that show you've done the work.

| # | Question | Coaching focus |
|---|---|---|
| 37 | Why do you want to leave your current role? | Forward-looking. What you're moving toward, not what you're running from. |
| 38 | Why this company specifically? | One or two specific signals that show you've done real research. Not "I love your culture." |
| 39 | Why this role specifically? | Connect your experience to the specific problem this role solves. |
| 40 | Where do you want to be in three years? | Direction, not a title. What you want to build or learn, not just what you want to become. |
| 41 | What does a great manager look like to you? | Specific behaviours. Not adjectives. "Someone who gives direct feedback on my work" beats "someone supportive." |
| 42 | What does success look like to you in this role in the first six months? | Concrete outcomes. Not activity (meetings, relationships). Impact. |
| 43 | What's most important to you in your next role? | Two things maximum. Specific enough that they couldn't apply to every job. |
| 44 | What do you know about us that made you want to apply? | Specific claim + why it matters to you. Not a company overview recitation. |

---

### Process and approach
*Default shape: Three Steps*
*Word target: 80–110 words*

These questions ask how you think and work. The failure mode is describing activity without logic or sequence. The coaching focus is three verb-led steps with clear progression — each one enabling the next.

| # | Question | Coaching focus |
|---|---|---|
| 45 | Walk me through how you'd approach your first 90 days in this role. | Listen → learn → act. Not a list of things you'd do. A sequence with logic. |
| 46 | How do you prioritise when everything feels urgent? | A system with steps. Not "I make a list." Three moves with verbs. |
| 47 | How do you approach a problem you've never seen before? | What you do first, then next. Process, not personality traits. |
| 48 | Walk me through how you make a difficult decision. | Three sequential steps. Each verb-led. |
| 49 | How do you approach building relationships with a new team? | Specific steps. Not "I'm a people person." |
| 50 | How do you manage your time and workload? | A real system in three steps. Not a list of tools. |

---

### Analytical and strategic
*Default shape: Conclusion → Support (analytical mode)*
*Word target: 80–110 words*

These questions ask you to reason out loud. The failure mode is presenting evidence without a claim, leaving the listener to infer your point. The coaching focus is leading with the conclusion, then explaining the logic, then providing evidence, then stating the implication.

| # | Question | Coaching focus |
|---|---|---|
| 51 | What's the biggest challenge you'd expect to face in this role? | State the challenge in sentence one. Then why you expect it. Then what you'd do about it. |
| 52 | What do you think this company/team is getting wrong right now? | Specific claim, not hedged observations. Reason, then one piece of evidence. |
| 53 | If you were starting this role tomorrow, what would you focus on first and why? | Conclusion first — name the thing. Then the reason. Then the implication. |
| 54 | What trends in this industry do you think matter most right now? | One or two specific trends, not a general survey. State why they matter for this company. |
| 55 | How do you evaluate whether a new initiative is worth pursuing? | A framework in three criteria or fewer. Each one specific enough to be testable. |

## Custom Questions

Custom questions are generated from user-provided scenarios during a session. They follow the same scoring and coaching process as interview questions.

When generating a custom question:
1. Extract or clarify the specific question being asked
2. Identify the question type using the categories above
3. Assign the default shape for that type
4. Set a word target consistent with that type
5. Identify the coaching focus based on the shape's discipline rules

Custom questions are logged to `coaching_state.md` with a `Custom` tag.

## Games Mode

Games are a separate practice mode from Interview and Custom. They don't involve questions or scoring — they build the underlying communication muscles that make answers better: flow, resilience, conviction, range, and recovery.

The coach should offer Games as a mode at session start. Games are particularly useful when the user wants to warm up before an interview, break out of a rut, or work on a root cause that question practice alone hasn't fixed.

**When to suggest Games over Interview practice:**
- User's Lead or Brevity scores are stuck despite repeated sessions — the issue may be a flow problem, not a structure problem
- User mentions nerves, anxiety, or feeling robotic
- User asks to warm up before a real interview
- User hasn't done a game session in five or more sessions

---

### Conductor
**What it trains:** Emotional range, flow state, trusting that energy leads and words follow.

**The mechanic:** A random topic is given. Speak continuously while numbers (1–10) flash on screen — each represents an energy intensity. Instantly match your speaking energy to each number as it appears. A "breathe" slide means pause completely until it passes, then continue.

**The insight:** Each energy level unlocks different thoughts, memories, and content. You're not saying the same thing louder or quieter — you're accessing genuinely different material. Low numbers require willingness to take up space and be still. High numbers require willingness to commit without certainty.

**What to watch for:** Most people have a comfort zone — strong at the highs, uncomfortable at the lows, or stuck in the middle. Where the discomfort is tells you something. Struggling to go low usually means discomfort with silence and taking up space. Struggling to go high usually means fear of commitment or looking foolish.

**To run without the app:** Ask someone to call out random numbers between 1 and 10 while you speak on any topic. Match your energy. Or type nine random numbers, read them to yourself one at a time at roughly ten-second intervals.

**Coaching focus:** After the rep, ask: where did it feel easy, where did it feel hard? Don't score. Identify the edge and name what it might reflect.

---

### Triple Step
**What it trains:** Resilience under distraction, staying on a single thread, recovering from the unexpected without losing the line.

**The mechanic:** A random topic is given. Speak for one minute. Six random unrelated words are injected at intervals — integrate each one into your speech as seamlessly as possible without breaking your train of thought. The goal: a listener who can't see the words should struggle to identify which were the injected ones.

**The insight:** The game forces you to hold a direction under pressure. The stronger your initial direction, the easier the words are to integrate — they work for you instead of derailing you. Weak direction means every new word starts a new thread. Strong direction means every new word bends to serve the one you're already on.

**Variants:**
- Start with four words instead of six if new to the game
- Integrate metaphorically, not just literally ("the internet of my mind" not just "the internet")
- Use as a brain warm-up before any high-stakes conversation — a few reps lights up the parts of the brain needed for real-time communication

**Coaching focus:** After the rep, ask: did the words start new thoughts, or get absorbed into one direction? If the former, practise choosing a stronger direction at the top before speaking.

---

### Conviction Prompts
**What it trains:** Executive presence, standing behind your words, reducing hedging and throat-clearing.

**The mechanic:** A random topic is given. Speak while integrating conviction phrases as they appear — the beginning of a sentence that you must complete out loud:
- "This matters a ton."
- "I genuinely believe that..."
- "It astonishes me when..."
- "Game changer."
- "What I want you to understand is..."
- "The thing most people miss is..."

**The insight:** The prompt puts you in a state. The content follows the state. When forced to say "I genuinely believe that...", you find you have something to genuinely believe. The game reveals that conviction is a posture you can choose, not just a feeling you wait for.

**What to watch for:** Hedging immediately after the prompt ("I genuinely believe that... well, in some cases...") defeats the exercise. The prompt must carry through. If the user hedges, name it and have them redo the rep.

**Coaching focus:** After the rep, ask: did the content feel more certain? Did anything surprising come out? Conviction prompts often surface insights the speaker didn't know they had.

### Game Session Flow

Games sessions are not scored the same way as practice sessions. Instead:

1. Ask which game the user wants to play, or choose based on their current coaching state
2. Give a random topic (generate one — avoid anything requiring specialist knowledge)
3. Run the rep
4. After the rep, ask one question: what felt easy, what felt hard?
5. Offer to run another rep on the same game or switch
6. After two or three reps, surface one observation about what the pattern might mean

Log game sessions to `coaching_state.md` with a `Games` tag. Note the game played and the observation made. Do not log dimension scores — games aren't scored that way.

**Good random topic prompts for games:**
Habits, Failure, Risk, Home, The best decision I ever made, Patience, What I've learned recently, The hardest conversation I've had, Momentum, Sleep, Saying no, Attention, Trust, The thing most people overlook, Competition, Starting over, Simplicity, What I'd tell my younger self, Time.

### Presentations
*Planned shapes: Rule of Three, Three Steps*
*Planned preparation methods: Accordion Method, Bow and Arrow*
- Stakeholder presentations
- Strategy decks
- Team updates
- All-hands talks

**Accordion Method** (stub — activates with Presentations mode): Prepare talks by speaking, not writing. Compress through time constraints (3min → 2min → 1min → 30sec), then expand back up. Going down strips to the essential. Coming back up, everything added is intentional. Result: talk is internalised, not memorised. No script — just one arrow and three bookmarks.

**Bow and Arrow** (stub — activates with Presentations mode): Identify the one thing you want the audience to remember (the arrow). Build supporting weight around it — a story, data point, or anecdote (the bow). Apply at macro level (whole talk) and micro level (each slide). Shift the question from "what do I want to say?" to "what do I want them to remember?"

*Planned shapes: Conclusion → Support (analytical), Three Steps*
- Project status updates
- Escalations
- Progress reports

## Warm-Up Drills

Optional exercises before a practice session. Particularly useful before a real interview or high-stakes conversation. The coach can offer these at session start if the user asks to warm up.

### Conductor
Random topic is given. Speaker must speak continuously while numbers (1–10) flash on screen, each representing an intensity or energy level. Match the energy as each number appears. A "breathe" slide means pause completely until it passes.

What it builds: accessing different emotional states on demand. Each energy level unlocks different thoughts and memories. Energy leads, emotion follows, words fill in the gap.

To run without the app: ask a friend to call out random numbers between 1 and 10 while you speak on a topic. Match your energy to each number.

### Triple Step
Random topic is given. Speak for one minute. Six random unrelated words are injected at intervals — integrate each one into your speech as seamlessly as possible without losing your thread.

What it builds: resilience against distraction, staying on a single direction under pressure, recovering gracefully from unexpected interruptions. Also a strong brain warm-up before interviews.

To run without the app: write six random words on cards. Have a friend hold them up one at a time as you speak.

### Conviction Prompts
Random topic is given. Speak while integrating strong conviction phrases as they appear: "This matters a ton.", "I genuinely believe that...", "It astonishes me when...", "Game changer."

What it builds: executive presence, standing behind your words, reducing hedging. The prompt puts you in a state of conviction — the content follows.

### Double Exhale
Before speaking: breathe in for three seconds, sneak in a tiny extra breath, then exhale for six seconds. The relaxation response is triggered during the extended exhale, not the inhale. One or two rounds is enough.

### Tongue Twister
Recite a short tongue twister immediately before speaking (e.g. "I slit a sheet, a sheet I slit..."). Requires intense focus to avoid stumbling — forces you out of future-catastrophising and into the present moment. Also warms up the vocal cords.

### Reframe
Before a high-stakes conversation: remind yourself that the physical symptoms of anxiety are identical to excitement. Use a simple mantra — "I have value to add" or "I am ready" — to reframe the sensation rather than suppress it.

## Question Selection Logic

Used by the coach during `practice` to choose the next question.

**First session:** Ask the user which mode they want. If Interview, ask if they want a specific question type or to let the coach choose.

**Returning sessions:** Check `coaching_state.md` for the lowest-scoring dimension and most frequent failure mode. Select a question type that targets it:

| Lowest dimension | Preferred question types |
|---|---|
| Lead | Openers, Strengths, Motivation |
| Shape | Behavioural (STAR), Process (Three Steps) |
| Sequence | Behavioural, Process |
| Brevity | Any — set word target to the lower bound of the range |

**Avoid repeating** the same question in consecutive sessions unless the user asks to retry it.

**Rotate question types** across sessions to ensure broad coverage. Flag to the user if a question type hasn't been practised after five or more sessions.
